*Recognising possible symptoms, finding clinical support and managing eczema, sensory needs and household treatment.*

*Information checked: 31 October 2026.*

> **LWL is a parent-led organisation, not a medical service. This guide helps you recognise possible symptoms and find clinical support. If you suspect scabies, contact a pharmacist or GP promptly for assessment and individual treatment advice.**

If you have never encountered scabies, you are unlikely to recognise it confidently from the first few spots. You may first notice unexplained itching, more scratching than usual or a child waking at night because they are uncomfortable.

Scabies is treatable and has nothing to do with poor hygiene. However, itching, disrupted sleep, treatment and laundry can create considerable difficulties for some families supporting children and young people with additional learning needs (ALN).

Recent Welsh health board notices have brought scabies to public attention [[1]](#ref-1). Reports of cases nearby do not mean every family needs treatment. The useful response is to recognise changes that deserve assessment and follow clinical advice if someone is identified as a close contact.

**Where to start:**

- **New, persistent itching or an unfamiliar rash:** contact a pharmacist or GP for assessment.
- **Painful, hot or swollen skin, rapid worsening, fever or feeling unwell:** seek urgent GP advice or call **NHS 111 Wales** [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3).
- **A close-contact notification:** follow its instructions, even if you have no symptoms.

## What might a parent notice first?
{: #early-signs }

Possible early clues include:

- A child scratching more than usual, particularly at night.
- New, small raised spots or an unfamiliar itchy rash.
- Scratching between the fingers, around the wrists, underarms, waist or groin.
- Another household member developing unexplained itching.
- Symptoms following known close contact with someone diagnosed with scabies [[1]](#ref-1), [[4]](#ref-4).

These clues are more useful together. They do not confirm scabies: eczema and other conditions can also itch and disturb sleep.

Sometimes fine, short lines called **burrows** are visible, with a small dot at one end. They can be difficult to find. **You do not need to see a burrow or recognise a fully developed rash before asking for help** [[4]](#ref-4).

A useful description for the pharmacist or GP might be:

> “This itching is new. They are waking to scratch, and there are small bumps around their wrists. It is different from their usual sensory discomfort.”

### Can you spot it immediately after contact?

Not reliably. Symptoms after a first infestation may take several weeks to appear, sometimes up to eight weeks [[4]](#ref-4).

Household members may therefore develop symptoms at different times. If a healthcare professional identifies someone as a contact who needs treatment, they should follow that advice even without itching.

## What is scabies, and how does it spread?

Scabies is caused by tiny mites that burrow into the outer layer of the skin. The skin’s reaction produces an itchy rash.

It usually spreads through prolonged, close skin-to-skin contact. Sharing bedding, clothing or towels can also spread it, although this is less common [[1]](#ref-1).

**Scabies is not evidence that someone or their home is dirty.** Extra bathing does not replace treatment.

If you receive a school, hospital or care-setting notification, read what it asks you to do. Being in the same building is not automatically the same as being a close contact.

## Is it scabies, eczema or sensory discomfort?
{: #other-explanations }

Sensory sensitivities and a skin condition can exist together. A child who usually dislikes seams, labels or touch can still develop a new skin problem.

Parents can help by explaining what has changed:

- How does this compare with their usual sensory discomfort, including how long it lasts?
- Are there new bumps, marks, sores or changes in texture?
- Is scratching affecting different areas?
- Is sleep more disturbed than usual?
- Are other people itchy, or has there been a close-contact notification?

These observations support an assessment; they are not a home diagnostic test.

Clinicians consider the history, examination, possible exposure and alternative explanations together [[5]](#ref-5). A person with eczema can also acquire scabies, so an existing diagnosis should not automatically explain away new symptoms [[6]](#ref-6).

| Existing condition or concern | What to tell the pharmacist or GP |
|---|---|
| **Atopic eczema** | Previous flare-ups, usual locations, dry or cracked patches and whether existing treatment is helping [[2]](#ref-2). |
| **Contact dermatitis** | Recent changes in soaps, detergents, wipes, creams or materials touching the skin. Burning or stinging may accompany itching [[3]](#ref-3). |
| **Possible scabies** | New night-time itching, where spots or burrows occur, relevant close contact and whether others are affected [[4]](#ref-4). |
| **Possible skin infection** | Increasing pain, warmth, swelling, discharge, pus, rapid worsening or feeling unwell. These need prompt medical advice [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3). |

Scratching can damage skin in several conditions, and more than one problem may be present.

### When a child cannot explain what is wrong

Notice changes in sleep, rubbing, scratching, dressing or tolerance of personal care. Describe differences from the person’s usual pattern.

Offer their normal communication methods, whether speech, writing, pictures, gestures or a communication device. Give them time to respond and avoid repeatedly suggesting a particular answer.

A short note of when symptoms started and which areas seem uncomfortable may help. You do not need an extensive diary before seeking advice.

Our [guide to finding ALN help in Wales](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/2026/07/where-to-go-for-aln-help-wales.html) provides wider navigation information. For a suspected skin condition, start with the appropriate pharmacist or GP.

## What does it look like on different skin tones?

On lighter skin, scabies spots may appear pink or red. On brown or black skin, redness may be less obvious, while spots or marks may look darker than surrounding skin. Raised bumps may be easier to feel than see [[4]](#ref-4).

Look for **changes from the person’s usual skin**, including texture, raised areas and scratch marks.

Eczema also varies with skin tone. Affected areas may look purple, grey, lighter or darker, as well as red [[2]](#ref-2). Colour alone cannot distinguish eczema from scabies.

The [NHS scabies page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/scabies/) includes photographs on lighter and darker skin. These illustrate possible appearances, but a rash does not have to match a photograph to deserve assessment.

If photographs do not show the problem clearly, explain that and ask about an in-person examination.

## Where to get help in Wales
{: #getting-help }

### Ask about the Common Ailments Service

Scabies is included in the Welsh **Common Ailments Service**, sometimes called the Common Ailments Scheme. Participating pharmacies provide free NHS advice and treatment where appropriate following a consultation [[7]](#ref-7), [[8]](#ref-8).

Find local pharmacies through [NHS 111 Wales Services Near You](https://111.wales.nhs.uk/localservices/).

Consider phoning first. Explain:

- Who has symptoms and their ages.
- Any known close contact.
- Whether there are babies or young children at home.
- Pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- Eczema, broken skin, allergies or other health conditions.
- Difficulties tolerating touch, creams or washing.

Ask how to arrange assessment and enough treatment for everyone who needs it. Do not assume one person’s supply will cover the household.

The pharmacist may recommend GP assessment or another prescribing service. The Welsh formulary identifies situations needing referral, including children under two, severe or infected skin, suspected crusted scabies and uncertainty about the diagnosis [[8]](#ref-8).

## What does treatment involve?
{: #treatment }

Treatment commonly involves a cream or lotion, often permethrin, with a repeat application usually needed seven days later [[1]](#ref-1), [[9]](#ref-9).

It needs to cover the areas specified by the clinician, rather than just visible spots.

Ask the pharmacist to explain:

- Which areas need treatment, including whether the face or scalp should be included.
- How much to use and how long to leave it on.
- What to do if hands or another treated area are washed.
- When to repeat treatment.
- How to coordinate treatment across the household.

**Follow the instructions for the particular product and person.** Age and clinical circumstances can affect the advice. Ask for clarification if packaging and clinical instructions appear to differ.

Routine household cases and outbreaks involving hospitals, care settings or crusted scabies may need different arrangements. Follow the clinical instructions given for your situation.

### Who else needs treatment?

Once scabies is diagnosed, household members and relevant close contacts generally need coordinated treatment, including people without symptoms [[1]](#ref-1), [[9]](#ref-9).

Tell the clinician about older relatives at home, shared living arrangements, overnight stays, hands-on carers and recent sexual contacts. Ask who needs treatment and how far back contacts should be considered.

A brief visitor is not necessarily in the same position as someone providing prolonged personal care. Let the healthcare professional help identify relevant contacts.

Coordinated treatment helps prevent people passing scabies back and forth. Informing contacts is a practical health conversation, not an accusation.

## Babies, pregnancy and older relatives
{: #family-members }

### Babies and children under two

**Contact the GP if a child under two has possible symptoms or lives with someone diagnosed with scabies.** Do not simply use an older child’s or adult’s pharmacy treatment on them [[4]](#ref-4).

Ask how their treatment should fit into the household plan.

### Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Tell the pharmacist or prescriber before treatment is selected so they can advise on a suitable product and its use [[9]](#ref-9).

### Older people and people needing care

Include older household members in the assessment even if they do not report itching.

In older people with cognitive impairment, symptoms may be difficult to recognise or communicate. Some do not show the usual scratching or rash pattern [[10]](#ref-10).

A less common form, **crusted scabies**, involves thickened, flaky or crusted skin and may cause little itching. It needs clinical assessment and a more specialised treatment plan [[10]](#ref-10).

Report unexplained skin changes rather than assuming an absence of itching rules scabies out.

## Eczema-prone or sensitive skin
{: #eczema }

Eczema can complicate assessment and treatment. Scabies, scratching and treatment irritation may all contribute to discomfort [[8]](#ref-8), [[9]](#ref-9).

NHS Wales advises GP assessment where scabies is suspected alongside another skin condition such as eczema [[4]](#ref-4).

Ask:

- Could this be eczema, scabies or both?
- Which treatment suits this person’s skin?
- How should usual moisturisers and prescribed eczema treatments fit around it?
- What can safely help itching afterwards?
- Which changes need reassessment?

Ask specifically whether and when to use any prescribed steroid cream during scabies treatment. Do not change an established treatment plan without clinical advice [[8]](#ref-8).

Do not add extra applications, dilute products or mix treatments to try to make them more effective or tolerable.

If treatment causes significant irritation or cannot be managed, contact the pharmacist or prescriber. Explain which step is difficult and ask for a review of the plan.

## Making treatment manageable for sensory needs
{: #sensory-needs }

These are practical planning suggestions, rather than a separate medical treatment protocol.

Tell the pharmacist before starting if touch, undressing, creams or washing are particularly difficult. Ask for written instructions and explain barriers to completing them.

A simple checklist could record:

- First treatment date and time.
- Areas to cover.
- Time to wash treatment off.
- Laundry arrangements.
- Repeat treatment date.

Where helpful, use a visual sequence, prepare comfortable clothing and offer choices about who helps and the order of steps, within treatment instructions.

Protect privacy, particularly for teenagers and young adults. Explain each step and allow time for questions.

Our article on [sensory needs and the ALN system](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/what-are-sensory-needs-aln-system/) provides wider background on recognising and explaining sensory needs.

**If part of the plan cannot be completed, say exactly which part and why.** Ask whether a GP or another prescribing clinician needs to review it, and request the agreed instructions in writing.

Do not quietly omit difficult areas or leave treatment incomplete.

## Laundry and favourite belongings
{: #laundry }

Coordinate laundry with treatment rather than delaying clinical help while trying to clean everything.

Welsh guidance advises cleaning bedding, clothing and towels used by affected people and relevant contacts. Hywel Dda recommends washing at **60°C or above** and sealing items that cannot be washed in a bag for **four days** [[1]](#ref-1), [[8]](#ref-8).

The Welsh formulary specifies at least 72 hours for bagged items. Follow the instructions given for your household, particularly during an outbreak.

Prepare clean clothes, towels and bedding for use after treatment. Ask the pharmacist to clarify which recently used items need attention and whether laundry precautions should be repeated alongside the second application. The Welsh sources checked do not give a single detailed schedule covering every household circumstance.

Check care instructions for specialist bedding and weighted blankets. Explain temporary changes before removing an important comfort item, and ask how to manage belongings that cannot tolerate a hot wash.

Tell the professional if laundry costs, facilities or limited spare bedding make the advice difficult to follow. Ask what to prioritise rather than assuming every item in every cupboard needs washing.

Avoid sharing towels, clothing and bedding during treatment. Follow advice about avoiding close physical contact until treatment is completed [[11]](#ref-11).

### Remember fire safety

The Welsh patient leaflet warns that treatment products and residues on clothing or bedding can create a fire risk. Keep away from smoking and naked flames, and read the product warnings [[11]](#ref-11).

## Do pets need treatment?
{: #pets }

**Human scabies is not caught from pets.** Your dog or cat does not need treatment simply because someone in the household has human scabies [[4]](#ref-4).

If an animal has its own itching or skin problem, contact a vet. Never use veterinary treatments on people or a person’s scabies medication on an animal.

## Can children attend school or nursery?
{: #school }

Tell the setting privately so it can follow appropriate guidance and support your child.

Public Health Wales guidance checked on **31 October 2026** says an affected person may attend if they can avoid close physical contact with others until **24 hours after the first treatment**.

Where a child cannot follow that advice because of age or additional needs, the guidance recommends staying away until 24 hours after the first treatment. It also calls for consideration of individual wellbeing and transmission risk [[12]](#ref-12).

**Having ALN does not automatically mean a child must stay home.** Discuss actual needs, including hands-on personal care.

Household contacts without symptoms do not automatically need exclusion, but still need treatment where advised [[12]](#ref-12).

Follow specific outbreak instructions from the setting and health team.

## What helps prevent spread?

Families do not need to buy treatment simply because cases have been reported nearby.

The main steps are to seek assessment for suspicious symptoms, follow close-contact notifications, coordinate treatment and complete the prescribed course. Follow the accompanying laundry and contact advice, and inform relevant carers or settings discreetly.

Extra washing alone cannot cure scabies. Treatment is needed [[8]](#ref-8).

If visiting a hospital or care setting, check current arrangements directly because restrictions can change.

## What if itching continues after treatment?
{: #further-help }

Itching can persist for several weeks after successful treatment. The skin’s continuing reaction and treatment irritation may contribute; itching alone does not prove that live mites remain [[9]](#ref-9).

Arrange review if itching continues two to four weeks after the final application, or earlier if symptoms worsen, new lesions develop or you are concerned [[4]](#ref-4), [[8]](#ref-8).

Do not keep repeating treatment without advice. A professional can assess irritation, reinfestation, application difficulties or whether a different treatment is needed.

Seek prompt assessment for an unusual crusted rash, particularly in someone with a weakened immune system [[10]](#ref-10).

For increasingly painful, hot or swollen skin, discharge or pus, rapid worsening, fever or feeling unwell, seek urgent GP advice or call **NHS 111 Wales** [[2]](#ref-2), [[3]](#ref-3).

## Start with a conversation

If you suspect scabies, contact a participating community pharmacy or your GP. Describe the symptoms, any known contact and any barriers to treatment. Ask what should happen next and when to seek further help.

You do not need to diagnose scabies yourself before asking for assessment.
