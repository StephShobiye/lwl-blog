*Information and service links checked: 20 November 2026. This article uses the report corrected on 21 October 2026.*

The October 2026 independent review, chaired by Professor Peter Fonagy, calls for a fundamental change in how people receive support for mental health difficulties, ADHD and autism. Its central message is straightforward: practical help should begin when someone needs it, alongside diagnostic assessment where appropriate, rather than being postponed until a diagnosis arrives [[1]](#ref-1).

For Welsh families, the question is whether this will make useful support easier to obtain before education breaks down, a young person becomes isolated or a parent carer has to leave work to hold everything together.

At Learn Without Limits CIC, prevention is the main focus of our work. We help families understand and navigate the system earlier. Our Bridge model is designed to provide temporary support when education has begun to break down, helping maintain learning and connection while longer-term arrangements are secured. Progress is the supported handover into provision delivered by the wider system. LWL does not replace statutory services [[2]](#ref-2).

The review strengthens the argument for earlier help. Whether it changes families’ experiences in Wales will depend on what follows.

## What the review proposes

Published on 9 October 2026 and corrected on 21 October, the review recommends a “needs-led plus diagnosis” approach. Support should reflect the person’s difficulties, circumstances and risk of deterioration. Diagnosis remains important, particularly where it informs treatment, but practical support and assessment should proceed in parallel [[1]](#ref-1), pp. 414–415.

For children and young adults, the proposals include:

- open-access, self-referral support hubs for **11–25-year-olds**;

- practical support, adjustments and monitoring while people await assessment;

- coordinated mental health and neurodevelopmental pathways;

- planned transitions between children’s and adult services;

- psychological therapies adapted for autistic people and people with ADHD;

- employment support integrated with mental health care [[1]](#ref-1), pp. 419–426.

The review also recognises a “missing middle”: young people whose needs exceed what ordinary services can manage, but who cannot access specialist support. It describes this as a gap in responsibility as well as provision [[1]](#ref-1), p. 419.

That matters. A family can receive several referrals without anyone taking responsibility for ensuring that useful help actually begins.

The age limits also need attention. The proposed youth hubs cover **11–25s**. The report does not extend that youth offer to 26-year-olds. Without a dependable onward pathway, extending youth provision could simply move the point at which support falls away [[1]](#ref-1), p. 423.

## Wales already supports many of these principles

This is principally a reform blueprint for England. Publication does not automatically change Welsh services.

Wales’s NEST/NYTH framework already promotes a “no wrong door” approach. It calls for services to respond to need and organise themselves around children and families, rather than expecting families to navigate fragmented referral systems [[3]](#ref-3).

The Welsh Mental Health and Wellbeing Strategy also says that transitions should be person-centred and needs-led, rather than determined solely by turning 18. It proposes exploring a specific approach for **14–25-year-olds with more complex needs** [[4]](#ref-4), p. 59.

Within education, Welsh Government guidance, updated on 4 November 2026, makes clear that a medical or neurodivergent diagnosis does not, by itself, determine whether a learner has additional learning needs. The statutory test asks whether a learning difficulty or disability calls for additional learning provision. Decisions must apply that test to the learner’s circumstances [[5]](#ref-5).

**Welsh families should not have to wait for this review to become policy before asking for learning needs to be considered.**

The challenge is translating existing principles into timely decisions, appropriate provision and support that survives transitions.

The October correction is relevant here. The review now says diagnosis has increasingly been **perceived** as a requirement for access to help. Families should not interpret that perception as a blanket legal requirement for educational support [[1]](#ref-1), [[5]](#ref-5).

## There are services, but families still need a workable route

Swansea already has early support and youth hubs that families may be able to access.

Platfform’s Swansea and Neath Port Talbot Hangout offers drop-in wellbeing support, activities and volunteering opportunities for **11–17-year-olds**. YMCA Swansea’s Y-HUB provides open-access youth work for **11–25s**, including individual support and outreach [[6]](#ref-6), [[7]](#ref-7).

These are local examples, rather than a Wales-wide service directory. Published service information was checked on 20 November 2026. It describes the offer, but does not establish current capacity, accessibility for a particular young person or how well handovers work in practice.

Different services have different purposes and boundaries. West Glamorgan’s Integrated Autism Service offers adult assessment and short-term support, but does not provide direct work with children, medication, crisis intervention or long-term care management [[8]](#ref-8).

A youth activity, an autism assessment and treatment for anxiety are different forms of help. Families may need more than one, with someone ensuring they connect.

For LWL’s prevention work, this is where clear information becomes practical support. Families need to know what a service provides, who can access it, how to approach it and what to do if it cannot meet the need.

## Will publication lead to change?

There are some concrete signals in England. The government’s initial response promises earlier support, stronger oversight of ADHD and autism assessments and consideration of changes to clinical guidance. NHS England intends new payment arrangements from April 2027, following consultation [[9]](#ref-9).

These are commitments to act. They are not evidence that every recommendation has been accepted or delivered.

Wales has its own work underway. The Neurodivergence Improvement Programme’s 2026–27 priorities include children’s assessment waits, national waiting-list standards and options for improving adult ADHD services, including shared care. These developments predate the review [[10]](#ref-10).

The Welsh mental health delivery plan also includes phased development of same-day, open-access psychological care and clearer primary care pathways [[11]](#ref-11).

Our assessment is that the review could strengthen pressure for earlier, coordinated support in Wales. **It does not itself guarantee shorter waits, a new local service or funding for community organisations.**

## What does the evidence actually support?

The review draws on research of different strengths. Some findings support specific interventions. Others show promising approaches that still need testing.

A trial of managed transitions found a small improvement in mental health outcomes from structured planning and information sharing. This supports purposeful handovers, but does not prove that changing service age limits alone will solve transition problems [[12]](#ref-12).

Research on Australia’s Headspace youth hubs reported improvements among people attending services. The study lacked a control group, so it cannot establish how much improvement was caused by the hub model [[13]](#ref-13).

An Autistica study of digital strengths-and-needs profiling involved 35 children aged 2–15. It examined feasibility and acceptability, rather than establishing that profiling caused better outcomes across the wider youth age range [[14]](#ref-14).

These distinctions matter for families. **A better description of need is useful only if it leads to a better response.**

The review itself warns that needs assessment must not become another eligibility hurdle or a mechanism for restricting care. Support before diagnosis should be additional help, not a reason to delay necessary assessment [[1]](#ref-1), pp. 403–406, 415.

## What families can ask for now

If learning or attendance is becoming harder, explain what has changed and how it affects access to education. Ask the school’s ALN coordinator or the responsible local authority to clarify what support can begin now, whether it is making an ALN decision, who is coordinating that process and when you should expect a response. Where an ALN decision is made, ask for the written decision and the relevant information about reconsideration or appeal [[5]](#ref-5).

A useful starting question is:

> “While assessment is continuing, what can we put in place now, who will coordinate it and when will we review whether it is helping?”

For a young person approaching a service age limit, ask which service will take over and who remains responsible during the handover. The review recommends planned transitions and support alongside assessment. It does not create a new Welsh entitlement simply through publication [[1]](#ref-1).

These questions help identify responsibilities. They do not transfer those responsibilities to families. Our [guide to finding ALN help in Wales]({% post_url 2026-07-21-where-to-go-for-aln-help-wales %}) provides further routes into advice and support.

## What would meaningful improvement look like?

For LWL, the most important changes would happen early:

- Families receive understandable information before difficulties become entrenched.

- Learning needs and practical adjustments are considered without unnecessary diagnostic delays.

- Referrals lead to help, with clear responsibility when services overlap.

- Changes in attendance, wellbeing or engagement prompt enquiry and support.

- Transitions preserve appropriate care and provision instead of requiring families to start again.

When education or training has already broken down, Bridge is designed to provide a temporary safety net against isolation and long-term disengagement. Statutory services remain responsible for their provision. A community safety net must not become an indefinite destination because suitable arrangements have not been secured [[2]](#ref-2).

Progress is a small part of LWL’s role: supporting handover into education, training or employment, alongside parent carers’ return to work where dependable arrangements make that possible.

The review offers a stronger basis for asking whether services are helping people participate in their lives, and supports the rationale for early navigation and temporary bridging. LWL’s own outcomes still need to be evaluated [[1]](#ref-1), [[2]](#ref-2).

**Will it help Welsh children, young adults and families? Potentially, yes. But the test is whether useful support arrives earlier and remains dependable, rather than whether families receive another assessment, another referral or another promise.**
