Part of the Learn Without Limits CIC knowledge base for ALN families in Wales.

![A welcoming Welsh school entrance on an autumn morning, with a child arriving with a parent and being greeted by a teacher. Sensory ear defenders are clipped to the child's backpack.](/assets/images/back_to_school_2026.png)

The beginning of a new school year can feel hopeful, chaotic and expensive all at the same time.

For families of children and young people with Additional Learning Needs, September can also bring a very practical question:

**Is the support my child needs in place, and is it working as intended?**

A new teacher, classroom, timetable, transport arrangement, peer group or school building can change how a child experiences education.

A child who coped well in July may struggle in September. Another child may surprise everybody by settling far more easily than expected.

That is why the first few weeks do not need to become a frantic attempt to solve every possible problem.

They can be a period of **watching, checking and recording what is actually happening**.

This guide gives Welsh parents a practical back-to-school check and links to more detailed Learn Without Limits CIC resources where you need them.

*This article provides general information only. It is not legal, clinical, therapeutic or safeguarding advice. If there is an immediate health, safety or safeguarding concern, do not wait to observe a pattern before seeking appropriate help.*

## Start with one simple question

Before worrying about whether everything is perfect, ask:

**Is my child able to access this school day safely, consistently and with the support they need?**

That question is wider than academic progress.

It may involve communication, sensory needs, anxiety, mobility, health, toileting, transport, peer relationships, attendance, fatigue, eating, transitions between lessons or simply whether the child understands what is expected of them.

If your child has moved between nursery, primary school, secondary school, college or another form of provision, our guide to [ALN transitions in Wales](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/2026/07/aln-transitions-wales-nursery-school-college.html) looks in more detail at why support may need to change when the environment changes.

## Check what was agreed against what is actually happening

If your child already has an Individual Development Plan, this is a good time to find it again.

You do not need to spend the first week interrogating everybody, but you do need to know what the plan says.

Check whether the Additional Learning Provision described in the IDP is actually happening in practice.

For example, if the plan says your child needs particular communication support, specialist input, small-group teaching, sensory adjustments or another form of provision, September gives you an opportunity to see whether that support has followed the child into the new term.

If you are not sure how a particular element is being delivered within the new timetable, it is reasonable to ask the school to explain.

If you need a refresher, see our [parent guide to IDPs in Wales](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/idp-in-wales-explained/).

If your child does not have an IDP but you or the school are beginning to suspect that they may have ALN, our guide to [what schools must do when they suspect ALN](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/2026/03/what-schools-must-do-aln-wales.html) explains the Welsh process in more detail.

## Notice the environment, not only the behaviour

A new term changes more than teachers and textbooks.

The sensory environment can change dramatically.

Different classrooms may mean different lighting, noise, smells, movement, seating, corridors, lunch arrangements and expectations.

Secondary school can add bells, crowded corridors, room changes, multiple teachers and greater organisational demands.

A child who comes home angry, exhausted, tearful or unable to speak about the day may be communicating that something about the school day is difficult to access or manage.

Something about the environment may be costing them more energy than adults realise.

For younger children, see [Sensory Needs in Primary School: What Parents in Wales Need to Notice, Record and Ask For](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/sensory-needs-primary-school-wales/).

For older pupils, see [Sensory Needs in Secondary School: Why Things Can Intensify in Years 7 to 11](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/sensory-needs-secondary-school-wales/).

## Pay attention to the journey as well as the school day

Sometimes the difficulty is not the classroom.

It is getting there.

A new transport route, longer journey, crowded bus, unfamiliar taxi, earlier start or complicated connection can affect whether the whole arrangement is sustainable.

The same applies to children who now have to move between different parts of a large school site or cope with a much more complicated timetable.

If something looks difficult, ask whether it is merely unfamiliar and likely to settle, or whether there is a structural problem that needs addressing.

A plan that works beautifully on paper may still fail if the child cannot realistically access it.

## Make sure health information has travelled with the child

September is also a useful time to check practical health arrangements.

If your child has medication, allergies, epilepsy, diabetes, continence needs, fatigue, mobility needs, feeding issues, mental health needs or another health condition affecting education, check that the relevant people have the current information.

Families with more complicated caring arrangements may also want to check who the school has recorded as contacts, who receives communication and who needs to be involved in planning.

Our article [Who Should Be Included in an IDP or Healthcare Plan in Wales?](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/2026/05/who-should-be-included-idp-ihp-wales.html) looks at this in more detail.

## Take early attendance difficulties seriously without assuming the worst

Some children need a little time to settle.

Others begin showing signs very quickly that the environment is not working.

Watch for patterns such as headaches, stomach aches, sleep disruption, panic before school, shutdown after school, increasing distress on Sunday evenings, refusal to enter particular lessons or exhaustion that grows across the week.

One difficult morning does not tell you everything.

A repeated pattern tells you more.

If attendance starts becoming difficult, our article on [sensory needs, attendance, EBSA and school avoidance](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/sensory-needs-attendance-ebsa-school-avoidance/) looks at why “won’t attend” can sometimes mean “can’t access”.

We also have separate [attendance guidance for families in Wales](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/2026/01/attendance-guidance-for-families-in.html).

## Keep a small record, not a second full-time job

Parents are often told to document everything. That can become exhausting very quickly.

You do not necessarily need a huge evidence bundle in the second week of September. A simple running record may be far more useful.

One practical option is to buy an **academic diary with enough space for both you and your child’s class teacher, form tutor or another agreed member of staff to make brief notes**, if the school is happy to use one.

Some schools refer to this as a home-school communication diary or communication book.

Keep the entries short. Bullet points are enough.

You might record:

- what happened
- whether it was a difficulty or a particularly good success
- roughly what time of day it happened
- the lesson, activity or general environment
- anything unusual about noise, crowds, transitions, demands or routine
- what appeared to help
- what appeared to make things harder

The aim is not to produce pages of commentary every day.

It is to build a simple picture over time.

This can also be useful to take to appointments with clinicians, therapists or other practitioners involved with your child. A record showing **when, where and under what circumstances things happen** may help professionals notice patterns that are difficult to identify from isolated incidents or from memory alone.

The diary is not diagnostic evidence, but it can provide useful real-world context for conversations with professionals.

It is equally important to record the good days.

Knowing that a child consistently manages well in one environment, with one type of support, with a particular member of staff or at a particular time of day can be just as informative as recording when things go wrong.

### A quick privacy note

A communication diary travelling backwards and forwards in a school bag may not be the right place for highly sensitive health, safeguarding or family information.

Use the school’s agreed secure communication route for information that should not be accessible to anybody who happens to pick up the diary.

If you are unsure what should go in the book, ask the school how they would like it to be used.

The purpose is not to build a case against the school.

It is to help parents, school staff and professionals answer a much more useful question:

**What patterns are we actually seeing, and what can we learn from them?**

## Ask calm, specific questions

If you need to contact school, specific questions are usually more useful than a general statement that your child is struggling.

For example:

> We have noticed that X is becoming very distressed after lunchtime and is increasingly anxious about returning to school the next morning. Could you let us know what happens around lunchtime and the transition back into afternoon lessons, and whether staff have noticed anything similar?

Or:

> X’s IDP says that they require [provision]. Please could you confirm how this is currently being delivered within the new timetable and who is responsible for coordinating it?

The objective is to understand what is happening before deciding what needs to happen next.

## You do not have to navigate every problem through the same door

One of the biggest difficulties for ALN families is being repeatedly told to “get support” without anyone explaining which support route fits which problem.

Some issues belong primarily with the school.

Some need the ALNCo.

Some may require local authority involvement.

Others may involve health evidence, social care, advocacy, transport or another professional route.

Our [Where to Go for ALN Help in Wales: A Parent Route-Map](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/2026/07/where-to-go-for-aln-help-wales.html) is designed specifically for families trying to work out which door to knock on next.

The Welsh Government has also produced an ALN Parent and Carer Toolkit. We explain what it contains and how families can use it in [The Welsh Government ALN Parent Toolkit Explained for Families](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/2026/07/welsh-government-aln-parent-toolkit-explained.html).

## Sometimes another parent knows the answer to the small question

Not every back-to-school niggle needs a professional intervention.

Sometimes what you need is another parent who has already dealt with the same practical problem.

Learn Without Limits CIC has a **nearly 1,000-member parent and carer community, with members from across Wales**, and that shared experience can be particularly useful during the first few weeks of term.

Questions can range from the very practical:

*Where did you find that specialist uniform item at a sensible price?*

At a time when many households are already dealing with back-to-school costs, other parents may also know where specialist clothing, equipment or practical items can be sourced more affordably.

The questions can also be about bigger worries, including:

- school transport
- transition between settings
- sensory difficulties
- communication with school
- attendance concerns
- equipment
- local services
- what other families have tried when something is not working

Peer support does not replace professional, educational, medical or legal advice.

But another family may be able to point you towards a useful question to ask, a service you had not heard of, a practical solution that worked for them or simply reassure you that somebody else understands why a seemingly small problem has become such a headache.

## September does not have to be perfect

The first few weeks of term are an adjustment period for children, parents and schools.

Not every difficulty means the placement is failing.

Not every good first week means everything will remain easy.

What matters is whether adults are noticing what the child needs, responding when difficulties emerge and adapting where necessary.

For parents, the aim is not to spend September anticipating every crisis.

It is much simpler:

**Observe. Record. Ask. Review.**

What was supposed to happen?

What is actually happening?

What is helping?

What is becoming harder?

What happens on the good days?

And if something does not look sustainable, what needs to change before a manageable problem becomes a crisis?

**That is prevention too.**
