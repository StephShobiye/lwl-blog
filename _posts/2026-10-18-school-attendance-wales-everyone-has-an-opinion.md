*Understanding attendance pressure, family expectations, peer influence and informed choices for children with ALN.*

When a child struggles to attend school, advice can arrive from every direction.

School may emphasise attendance. Relatives may recommend firmer boundaries or question whether qualifications matter. Other parents may suggest changing settings or considering home education. Online, someone may sound certain that their approach is the answer.

The challenge is deciding which advice fits your child, while making sure the people responsible for support act on what is needed.

At Learn Without Limits CIC, our aim is to help parents make informed choices. That includes understanding evidence, Welsh rights and responsibilities, the child’s experience and the practical consequences of different options.

Sometimes it means choosing a different path from people around you. Sometimes it means reconsidering a decision when new information becomes available.

But informed parents cannot compensate for missing provision or unavailable services. Attendance procedures should be evaluated for whether they improve access to education and engagement with families, including whether they inadvertently make either harder.

## Similar attendance figures can hide different problems

A child absent because of illness, a child frightened of bullying and a teenager skipping lessons with friends may have similar attendance percentages. They may need very different responses.

Several difficulties can overlap. A young person may have health needs, struggle in lessons and worry about friendships. Their experience may change over time.

An attendance record can identify a pattern that needs attention. It cannot, by itself, explain that pattern.

The same caution applies to research. Findings about unexplained absence, described as truancy in some studies, cannot automatically explain disability-related absence or severe school distress.

Before deciding how to respond, adults need to understand what is preventing attendance, what education the child can currently access and what support has actually been tried.

## When following the process stops solving the problem

Consider this illustrative situation.

A parent reports that crowded transitions between lessons are overwhelming their child. Staff arrange an earlier exit, but it is inconsistently available. The quieter route also leads to a classroom where the child is still struggling.

Attendance remains low. Further reminders arrive while the family and staff disagree about whether the adjustment has been properly tried.

Everyone may be making an effort. The support may still be incomplete.

If subsequent communication repeatedly asks the parent to improve attendance without resolving those questions, the family may feel unheard. Staff, meanwhile, may feel that their efforts are being dismissed.

This is a possible pattern to investigate, not a proven sequence explaining every attendance difficulty. It illustrates why counting contacts or recording that support was “offered” cannot establish whether the child received effective help.

Accurate records and follow-up remain important. The question is what happens after a difficulty has been identified.

Our earlier article, [When Everyone Is Acting Rationally, and Children Are Still Harmed](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/2026/01/when-everyone-is-acting-rationally-and.html), explores how organisational processes can produce harmful outcomes even where individuals are trying to fulfil their roles.

For attendance work, useful scrutiny includes asking whether the process itself has become another barrier to communication or progress.

## What the research supports

The research gives us reasons to take relationships seriously, but it does not establish one approach that reliably works for every child.

A substantial 2026 systematic review involving Swansea University researchers examined 61 articles reporting 57 randomised trials. Parental engagement interventions produced small average improvements in attendance, particularly among younger children. These interventions included attendance messages and more involved family support. Most trials were conducted in the United States. [1]

That finding challenges the idea that attendance communication is inherently harmful. Accurate, relevant information can help.

It does not prove that trusting relationships alone produced the improvement, or that sending more messages will help a family whose difficulties remain unresolved. The interventions differed, and results varied considerably.

A 2025 UK review focused specifically on school-partnered approaches to emotionally based school avoidance. Only five studies remained after quality assessment. The authors could not establish intervention effectiveness because the evidence was too limited and methodologically weak. A punitive educational culture was a recurring theme in the studies reviewed. [2]

A separate 2026 review of 37 studies highlighted family–school partnerships, parental agency, relationships and access to support. [3]

These findings support careful collaboration and evaluation. Neither a firm instruction to attend nor a compassionate description of an intervention establishes that it will address a particular child’s difficulty.

## Support must reach the actual barrier

If attendance difficulty involves bullying, sensory overload, inaccessible learning or unmet health needs, reminders cannot resolve those barriers by themselves.

A 2025 UK study interviewed 30 neurodivergent young people experiencing school distress and attendance difficulties. They described interacting problems involving sensory differences, uncertainty, social challenges and poor understanding of neurodivergence. Some wanted to attend and learn but struggled with the environment. [4]

The study helps explain why adults need to hear directly from children wherever possible. It does not mean every young person will offer a clear explanation immediately.

A child might communicate through conversation, writing or pictures. Changes in behaviour and observations over time may also help adults understand their experience. What happens before school, during the day and afterwards all deserves attention.

Our article [Sensory Needs, Attendance, EBSA and School Avoidance: When Won’t Attend May Mean Can’t Access](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/sensory-needs-attendance-ebsa-school-avoidance/) explores this distinction.

A useful plan identifies the difficulty, what will change, who will act and when progress will be reviewed. It should distinguish between support discussed, support offered and support received.

If an adjustment has not worked, the next question is why. Was it delivered consistently? Did it address the right difficulty? Did it create another problem? Has something else changed?

## Rebuilding trust after communication breaks down

A family and school may reach a point where every message is read through earlier disagreements. Another meeting will achieve little if it repeats the same positions without resolving outstanding actions.

The following are LWL’s practical recommendations for a reset, rather than a tested intervention package.

Start with a short shared record of what is known, what is disputed and what remains unresolved. Agreement on every explanation may not be possible immediately. Adults can still establish whether an adjustment happened, whether a referral was made and who is responsible for the next action.

Correct factual errors in records, and distinguish observations from interpretations. “The parent declined the proposed meeting time” conveys different information from “the parent refuses to engage”.

Agree a manageable communication arrangement, including a named contact and a way to raise urgent concerns. Explain which attendance or safeguarding contacts remain necessary and why, so families understand what to expect.

Record what each service will do, alongside any agreed family actions. If support is delayed, explain the interim arrangements and how the delay will be escalated.

Trust does not have to be restored before practical work can begin. Delivering a clear promise, reporting honestly when something cannot happen and following up an unresolved problem give people something concrete on which to rebuild.

## Teenage friendships can pull in different directions

Friendships deserve attention without becoming an easy explanation for absence.

Researchers distinguish between **selection**, where young people choose friends with similar experiences or behaviour, and **influence**, where their behaviour changes through those relationships.

A longitudinal study of 342 adolescents aged 14–15 found evidence of both processes in unexplained absence. [5]

Similar attendance within a friendship group therefore cannot automatically be attributed to one child influencing the others. Young people may already share difficulties or experiences that help explain why they became friends.

Friendship can also support attendance. A Finnish study of 838 pupils, published in 2026, found that greater perceived closeness with best friends at school was associated with less truancy at both measurement points during the first year of lower secondary school. Other findings varied by absence type and time of year. [6]

These studies help us ask better questions about friendships. They cannot determine why a particular Welsh child with ALN is absent.

For an individual teenager, LWL suggests exploring several possibilities. Friends may share unresolved barriers, encourage learning or help maintain a connection with school. Friends’ experiences and expectations may also shape how a young person sees attendance or a possible return.

Helpful questions might include:

- Who helps you feel understood?
- How do they respond when you want to try something different?
- If the original difficulty were resolved, would you want to attend?
- What would help you stay connected to friends while taking your next step?

Our article [Sensory Needs in Secondary School: Why Things Can Intensify in Years 7 to 11](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/sensory-needs-secondary-school-wales/) considers the wider environment around those relationships.

Supporting independence should include helping a young person maintain safe connections, particularly when their next step differs from their friends’.

## Parents need support and room to disagree

A 2025 study involving 947 parents of children with current or previous school distress, alongside comparison groups, documented substantial strain and experiences of blame or disbelief. Online support groups were the most consistent source of support identified by parents experiencing school distress. [7]

Peer support can provide understanding, practical suggestions and questions a parent had not considered. For someone who feels repeatedly dismissed, being believed can matter enormously.

But another family’s experience cannot determine your child’s next step.

In the research reviewed for this article, we did not find strong direct evidence establishing that parent support groups cause persistent absence. That does not establish that groups never influence decisions.

Whether advice comes from a local parent group, a conversation at the school gate or an online community, LWL suggests asking:

- Can families describe positive school experiences and question professional advice?
- Are claims checked against current sources and Welsh arrangements?
- Can people choose different routes, disagree or change their minds and still receive support?
- Does the discussion identify practical options, or make one answer feel compulsory?

These are suggestions for assessing advice, rather than findings from a study of parent-group influence. They apply to LWL too.

[Where to Go for ALN Help in Wales: A Parent Route-Map](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/2026/07/where-to-go-for-aln-help-wales.html) explains how peer support, official services, advocacy and specialist advice can contribute in different ways.

## When the pressure comes from your own family

Advice from relatives can carry particular weight. These are people you may love, rely on for practical help or find difficult to disagree with.

Family expectations can pull in very different directions.

“All his cousins had perfect attendance and became doctors. You need to be firmer.”

“Why does he need exams? He’ll work on the farm or in the family business anyway.”

These are illustrative examples, not research quotations. This section offers LWL’s practical guidance.

Both examples place an expectation about the child’s future ahead of understanding their present circumstances.

Comparisons with cousins or siblings tell us little about whether this child can access education safely and consistently. Another child’s attendance record does not explain their health, support, opportunities or route into a profession.

Equally, a possible place in a family business should not close down a young person’s choices before they have explored them.

Farming, trades and family businesses can offer skilled, rewarding futures. The question is whether that route reflects the young person’s interests and offers suitable learning and development. Qualifications, training and practical experience may all contribute.

Pressure can also run the other way, with relatives dismissing vocational ambitions because they expect university or a particular profession.

Relatives may speak from affection, worry or their own experiences. Their intentions can be caring while their advice still misses the child’s needs.

You do not have to persuade every relative before seeking support. A specific request may be more useful than another argument:

“Please let them explain what they want before suggesting a career.”

“We need encouragement with the agreed plan, without comparisons with their cousins.”

The aim is to preserve useful support while keeping the child’s needs and future opportunities in view.

## Weighing conflicting advice in practice

Imagine a fictional Year 10 learner who wants qualifications but is struggling with crowded corridors and unpredictable lessons.

School recommends an immediate full return. A relative says the parent should be stricter. Someone in an online group recommends home education. A friend who is also absent says returning will not help.

The parent and young person do not have to accept any one account in its entirety.

The young person chooses to write down which parts of the day feel manageable, where difficulties arise and what they want to preserve, including particular subjects and friendships. They identify questions they want answered and decide whether to attend the meeting, contribute in writing or ask a trusted adult to help communicate their views.

The discussion can then separate the issues. Which barriers have been identified? What adjustments have actually happened? What would the proposed return involve? What education and support would be available under another arrangement?

Adults remain responsible for checking options, arranging provision and explaining decisions. The young person should not have to design their own support package or settle disagreements between services.

Any agreed next step needs clear responsibilities and a review date. At review, the young person’s account should help establish whether the plan worked in practice.

This example does not prescribe a return or a move out of school. It shows how competing opinions can become questions requiring evidence and practical answers.

## Being brave enough to choose differently

Swimming against the tide does not have to mean making a dramatic decision.

It might mean trying a carefully supported return when friends believe school can never work. It might mean seeking different provision when relatives insist your child simply needs to attend.

It might mean supporting a vocational ambition when the family expects university, or keeping further study open when others assume a family job is settled.

The same care applies to widely shared online advice. Popularity cannot establish whether a claim is accurate, current, applicable in Wales or suitable for your child.

You can value someone’s support and still say:

“I understand why that worked for your family. We need to check whether it meets our child’s needs.”

You do not need to become an expert before asking for help. Useful advice should identify what information is missing and who can provide it.

## Relief now and a sustainable education plan

A decision may reduce distress immediately while leaving questions about learning, friendships, qualifications or family capacity unresolved.

Equally, improved attendance may conceal continuing distress or exhaustion.

Both situations need review. LWL suggests separating three questions.

**What helps now?**

What needs attention to address immediate distress, health, safety or access difficulties?

**What comes next?**

What suitable education, support and social connection will the child receive? What does the child want?

**What makes it sustainable?**

Who will provide the arrangement? What resources does it require? What happens if it stops working?

Recovery cannot be reduced to a fixed timetable. Adults should still agree review dates, maintain appropriate contact and check what education and support the child receives.

Progress should be considered against the child’s starting point. Alongside attendance, a review might examine access to lessons, participation in learning, distress, recovery after school, relationships and progress towards the young person’s goals.

A reassuring attendance figure should not end that enquiry. Nor should continuing absence mean other improvements are ignored. The review needs to identify what remains unresolved and what action follows.

Our guides to [Types of Educational Provision in Wales for ALN Pupils](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/2026/07/types-educational-provision-wales-aln-pupils.html) and [EOTAS vs Home Education in Wales](https://blog.learnwithoutlimitscic.org/2026/03/eotas-vs-home-education-wales.html) explain different arrangements.

Sometimes a family is choosing between inadequate options because suitable provision is unavailable. That is a service problem requiring action, not evidence that the parent has failed to choose well.

## What should institutions change?

Welsh Government’s attendance guidance recognises engagement, relationships and responses adapted to children and families. [8]

Estyn’s September 2026 review of Education Welfare Services identifies the value of relationships and early support, alongside capacity and funding problems. It reports variable and often limited impact from enforcement where absence involved complex circumstances, wellbeing concerns or what the report calls “entrenched disengagement”. [9]

Reduced contact alone cannot explain why a family has disengaged. Their experience of previous contact should inform the response.

Recognition already exists. The question is how consistently it changes practice.

LWL proposes that evaluation should ask:

- When a barrier was identified, what changed?
- Was agreed support delivered before its effectiveness was judged?
- Were the child’s views gathered accessibly?
- Did warnings continue while agreed actions remained outstanding?
- Did the approach help engagement, or did its timing, tone or repetition create further barriers?
- What was learned when an intervention did not help?

These are LWL’s proposed questions, not an existing inspection checklist.

Estyn can evaluate and report. Schools, local authorities and other services have different delivery responsibilities. Welsh Government shapes policy and funding.

Where several services are involved, LWL believes families should know who is coordinating the plan, which actions belong to each service and how outstanding actions will be escalated.

Welsh Government has accepted recommendations to consolidate attendance guidance and strengthen data systems. Sustainable multi-year funding was accepted in principle. [10] The practical test is what families receive.

If formal correspondence arrives, seek advice promptly about the document and its deadlines. Asking for support does not suspend a formal process.

## Keeping your child’s future in view

LWL’s emphasis is prevention, helping families understand difficulties and seek support before disconnection becomes prolonged.

The best next step may differ between children. It should be informed by their experience, relevant evidence, practical circumstances and the support needed to make it work.

A useful attendance response should leave everyone clearer about what is preventing access, what will change and who will act.

Parents should be supported to question advice and reconsider options. That includes choosing differently from people around them, without losing access to support.




## A note on the evidence

Recent publication does not always mean recently collected evidence. Reference [1] searched new research up to October 2024 and incorporated earlier trials. Reference [6] used Finnish data collected in 2015–2016.

Interview and parent-experience studies explain experiences but cannot establish their prevalence across all families. Reference [7] used a self-selected online sample and included retrospective accounts. Observational friendship studies cannot eliminate every alternative explanation.

The sections on rebuilding trust, assessing advice, family expectations and reviewing plans offer LWL’s practical recommendations. They should not be read as evidence that a particular family relationship causes absence or that a proposed approach is proven to work.
